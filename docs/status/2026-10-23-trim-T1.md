# T1: trim the child instead of growing the parent. 23 Oct 2026

Prompt 57. Exploratory; nothing is adopted. The design is Aviyah's. Readings, stability, recall and
the other evaluation sections are not run.

**Both declared tests pass.** With the flip off the code reproduces frozen O50s **byte for byte** on
all four tables, with **0 members removed** and 0 themes dropped. On fixtures the flipped rule never
adds a member to any theme, the downward propagation reaches a theme contained in the trimmed one,
every final edge is strict containment, and a theme trimmed below 3 members is dropped.

**The flip works as specified and is very expensive.** It removes **53,951 members**, drops
**3,788 themes** below the minimum size, and leaves **3,456 themes against O50s's 5,658** -- a 39%
loss. Only **9.1% of O50s's themes have a T1 counterpart at J >= 0.9**, and of O50s's 12,555 edges
only **207 even have both endpoints matched**, of which 112 survive. T1 is not a variant of O50s;
it is a different ontology.

The one thing it does not break is coverage: **all 5,539 pathways remain in at least one theme, and
every pathway T1 removed from some theme is still in another one -- zero orphaned.**

"Trimmed by more than one parent" counts themes with two or more *direct* trim events. A theme
cannot be trimmed twice by the same partner -- once it loses its single stray it is nested in that
partner and rule 2 cannot fire on the pair again -- so direct events and distinct trimming parents
are the same count.

Themes losing more than 10% and more than 25% of their **gate-exit** members, which is the

**The damage is concentrated entirely in the small bands, and it is arithmetic, not accident.** A
3-member theme losing one member loses 33% and falls under the minimum; a 6-member theme loses 17%.
Above 100 pathways not one theme loses even 10%. So the flip is cheap for large themes and fatal for
small ones -- the exact opposite of inheritance, which piles its cost onto the giants.

**The indirect effects are large and mostly follow from themes vanishing.** Rule-2 events fall by
33,152 because trimming removes the strays that would have triggered later events, and because
3,788 themes stop existing. Chain collapses fall by 594 -- a 78% drop -- which is the clearest
indirect signal: inheritance was *creating* most of the near-chains that collapse later, exactly as
prompt 56 Part A found for twins. Stale merges rise slightly, by 21.

Rule 4 is 0 in both, which is worth stating plainly: in this configuration consensus never reaches
the coexist rule, because every pair that is not nested, inherited or superseded has no overlap at
all and is skipped before rule 4 can apply.

## What this says

1. **The flip is implementable and strictness is preserved** -- the fixture checks that directly,
   and the byte-for-byte reproduction with the flip off proves nothing else moved.
2. **It costs 39% of the themes and most of the structure.** 3,788 themes die, 4,851 lose more than
   a quarter of their members, 9.1% of O50s's themes survive recognisably, and only 112 of 12,555
   edges are still edges.
3. **The cost falls on exactly the themes inheritance protects.** Inheritance pays for strictness by
   growing the big themes; trimming pays by destroying the small ones. Above 100 pathways no theme
   loses 10%; at 3-5 every trimmed theme loses more than 25%. The two directions are not symmetric
   alternatives -- they move the cost between size bands.
4. **Chain collapses fall 78%**, which independently confirms prompt 56 Part A from a second
   direction: inheritance was manufacturing most of the near-chains the repair later collapses.
5. **The giants are not cured.** 5 -> 8, with the 2,809-member theme now surviving.

**Nothing is adopted.** Whether a 3,456-theme ontology with a median depth of 3 is better or worse
than O50s is not measured here, and no theme was read.
